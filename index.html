CHAPTER 01

I trained to close the books. Then I met the software that closes them.

A B.Com in Professional Accounting teaches you what a trial balance has to prove. It does not teach you what happens when six companies each keep their own chart of accounts and someone needs a group number by Friday.

I moved into ERP consulting in 2024 and found the two halves fitted together. Configuration questions turn out to be accounting questions wearing different clothes. When a client asks whether a cost should post to the project or the department, no amount of system knowledge answers it. You have to know what the number is for.

Three implementations later, the pattern is consistent. The hard part is never the software. It is getting six people who each believe they are already right to agree on one definition, and then holding that definition when the schedule gets tight.

Configuration you can redo. A wrong opening balance follows you for years.

CHAPTER 03

Teaching software to do the parts of accounting nobody enjoys

In 2025 my team began building Copilot agents inside Business Central. I designed the functional processes, wrote the specifications, tested every one and documented what came out.

The interesting agents were never the ones that summarise a record. They were the ones that had to form a judgement and then act on it inside a live financial system.

The one I would show you first

The Inventory agent runs over the item list, filters to items replenished by purchase, finds every item below its reorder point, and raises purchase orders using the vendor on the item card and the reorder quantity. Where several shortfall items share a vendor, it consolidates them onto one purchase order rather than raising four. Then it returns the URL of what it created, so a human can go and check.

That last detail matters more than the automation. An agent that acts inside a ledger and does not tell you exactly what it did is a liability, whatever time it saves.

CHAPTER 04

Then I wrote down everywhere the agents failed

Building them was the assignment. Documenting where they could not be trusted was not. I did it because we were about to put these in front of finance users, and finance users do not get to be surprised by their own ledger.

The terminal below is a faithful reconstruction of what I recorded in testing. Every response is a real failure mode. Try the last one twice.

FINDING 01
PROMPT BRITTLENESS

It only worked when the prompt was phrased one exact way

Any deviation failed. Natural phrasing failed. Which means it was not usable by the people it was built for — a demo, not a tool.

FINDING 02
SILENT SUCCESS

It reported an error while the record updated correctly

The write succeeded in Business Central and the interface said it had not. In a finance system that is worse than plain failure: the user retries, and now you have two.

FINDING 03
NON-DETERMINISM

Identical requests produced different outcomes

The same correctly formed update ran cleanly on one attempt and failed on the next with nothing changed. You cannot write a control around behaviour that will not repeat.

FINDING 04
CONSOLIDATION INSTABILITY

Merging separate agents into one made all of them less reliable

Individually the customer, vendor and item agents behaved. Combined into a single master agent, update and delete began routing to the wrong flow.

A demo shows you what a system does when it works. Somebody has to write down what it does when it doesn't.
WHY THIS CHAPTER EXISTS
