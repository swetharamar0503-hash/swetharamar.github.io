Entry 01Opening balance

Where this starts

I trained to close the books. Then I met the software that closes them.

A B.Com in Professional Accounting teaches you what a trial balance has to prove. It does not teach you what happens when six companies each keep their own chart of accounts and someone asks for a group number by Friday.

I moved into ERP consulting in 2024 and found the two halves fitted. Configuration questions are accounting questions wearing different clothes. When a client asks whether a cost should post to the project or the department, no amount of system knowledge answers that — you need to know what the number is for.

That is the work I do: sit between the finance team who know what they need and the developers who can build it, and make sure nothing is lost crossing the gap. Requirements, process maps, configuration, test cases, user manuals, and the unglamorous business of chasing a two-rupee variance until it explains itself.

Entry 02Six into one

Multi-entity implementation

Six companies that had never seen each other's numbers

Role Functional analyst, finance workstream Platform Dynamics 365 Business Central Scope 6 legal entities

The group ran six legal entities. Each closed its own books competently. Nobody could produce a consolidated position without a fortnight of spreadsheets, and by the time it arrived it was already describing the past.

The work was not technical first. It was agreeing what a shared chart of accounts had to look like when six finance teams each believed theirs was already correct — and each had reasons, because each had built around a real constraint.

What I did

  • Designed the shared Chart of Accounts and the dimension structure that let each entity keep its own reporting detail without breaking group-level roll-up
  • Configured General Ledger, Fixed Assets and Budgets across all six entities
  • Built the intercompany consolidation setup so group reporting ran from entity ledgers rather than from a spreadsheet
  • Migrated and validated opening balances — GL, fixed asset acquisition and depreciation, customer and vendor ledgers
  • Trained the users in every entity and ran UAT through to sign-off
The reconciliation model was the real deliverable. Configuration you can redo. A wrong opening balance follows you for years.

Before cutover I built reconciliation models in Excel that tested every migrated balance against source. Not a sample — every balance. It took longer than anyone had scheduled for it, and it was the reason go-live day was quiet.

Outcome

Group-level consolidated financial reporting produced from entity ledgers for the first time. Zero reconciliation discrepancies at day-one go-live across all six entities.

Entry 03Agent build

Applied AI in ERP

Teaching software to do the parts of accounting nobody enjoys

Role Functional design and evaluation Stack Copilot Studio, Power Automate, MCP, Business Central APIs Built ~20 agents

In 2025 my team started building Copilot agents inside Business Central. I designed the functional processes, wrote the specifications, tested every one, and documented what came out.

The interesting agents were not the ones that summarise a record. They were the ones that had to make a judgement and then act on it inside a live financial system.

The one I would show you first

The Inventory agent runs over the item list, filters to items replenished by purchase, finds every item sitting below its reorder point, and raises purchase orders using the vendor on the item card and the reorder quantity. Where several shortfall items share a vendor, it consolidates them onto one purchase order instead of raising four. It returns the URL of what it created so a human can go and check.

That last detail matters more than the automation. An agent that acts inside a ledger and does not tell you exactly what it did is a liability, whatever it saves you.

Outcome

Working agents across master data, purchasing, sales, inventory, expenses and projects — each with a written process specification, a tested prompt sequence and a documented failure mode.

Entry 04Constraints

The report nobody asked for

Then I wrote down everywhere the agents failed

Building the agents was the assignment. Documenting where they could not be trusted was not. I did it because we were preparing to put these in front of finance users, and finance users do not get to be surprised by their own ledger.

Finding 01 — Prompt brittleness

The agent only worked when the prompt was phrased one exact way

Any deviation failed. Natural phrasing failed. Which means it was not usable by the people it was built for — a demo, not a tool.

Finding 02 — Silent success, loud failure

The agent reported an error while the record updated correctly

The write succeeded in Business Central and the interface said it had not. In a finance system that is worse than plain failure: the user retries, and now you have two.

Finding 03 — Non-determinism

Identical requests produced different outcomes

The same correctly formed update ran cleanly on one attempt and failed on the next, with nothing changed. You cannot write a control around behaviour that is not repeatable.

Finding 04 — Consolidation instability

Merging separate agents into one made all of them less reliable

Individually the customer, vendor and item agents behaved. Combined into a single master agent, update and delete operations began routing to the wrong flow.

A demo shows you what a system does when it works. Somebody has to write down what it does when it doesn't.

I closed the report with a plain assessment: capable of basic operations, not yet dependable for production finance use, with named reasons. That conclusion was less comfortable than the demo. It was also the only useful thing to say.

Why this is here

Every analyst can list what they built. This is the piece of work I would rather be judged on — knowing what a system cannot do, and being willing to write it down before someone else finds out the expensive way.

Entry 05Handover

Documentation and enablement

The manual is the part of the project that outlives you

For an international development nonprofit I configured finance, bank reconciliation, purchasing and sales against grant-funded accounting rules, then wrote the timesheet and leave management manuals — one for users, one for administrators, revised through four versions as the process settled.

Writing them properly meant walking each path myself: what defaults when a salaried resource creates a biweekly timesheet, what happens to dimensions inherited from the resource card versus the project card, what a user sees when they pick a dimension value that has already expired. Those are the questions that generate support tickets for the next three years if the manual is vague.

  • Timesheet and leave management user guides, revised across four versions
  • Separate administrator process documentation for the support team
  • Asset management user manual and module configuration guides
  • Functional test case suites covering finance, payables, receivables and reversals
  • Payment file field mapping for a US bank ACH format, working from the NACHA specification
  • Tenant migration runbook and checklist for a cross-tenant environment transfer
Outcome

100% quality management audit score across three consecutive projects, and a "Rising Star" award from my employer. Both came from documentation discipline rather than heroics.
